In a neighbourhood, a group of boys had crossed the threshold of childhood and entered their early teenage years. Yet, somehow, education had remained a distant part of their lives. They spent their days wandering through the streets, playing games, stealing mangoes and jujubes from nearby gardens, and finding amusement in all sorts of mischief. Their childhood was passing, but the opportunities that should have accompanied it were slipping away.



It would be unfair to judge them too harshly for their mischief. After all, childhood has its own language of rebellion, curiosity and adventure. What was more troubling was the absence of something that could help them understand the world beyond their immediate surroundings. They had little interest in books or classrooms. For them, studying was an obligation without purpose, something that took away time from the pleasures they understood. Stories, however, were different. They could listen to them for hours.



Perhaps recognising this, an older young man from the neighbourhood decided to approach them in a different way. He knew that telling them to study would hardly make them interested in education. So, one afternoon, he called the boys together and announced that he had a story to tell.



The announcement was enough to bring smiles to their faces.



"What is the story about, Dada?"



"What happens in it?"



He smiled and answered with a little mystery in his voice: "A hidden treasure."



The words immediately caught their attention. A treasure! Something buried, something lost, something waiting to be discovered. Their imaginations were already at work.



He began narrating his story. After a while, however, he stopped and asked them a question.



"Tell me, what do you think a hidden treasure really is?"



The answers came almost immediately.



"Gold!"



"Diamonds!"



"Silver!"



"Lots of money!"



He listened to them with an amused smile. "Interesting. So, all of you are looking for something valuable."



Then he reached for a book and placed it before them. It was Barnaparichay, the familiar Bengali primer through which generations of children had taken their first steps into the world of letters.



The boys looked at the book with a mixture of curiosity and disappointment. They had gathered to hear about a treasure, after all, and this was hardly what they had expected.



"What is this?" he asked.



"An A, Aa book," some of them replied.



"And how many of you can read it?"



A brief silence followed. Only one or two raised their hands. The others looked at one another, uncertain of what to say.



The young man then asked another question, one they had probably heard many times before.



"What do you want to become when you grow up?"



Their enthusiasm returned. One wanted to become a teacher, another a doctor, and someone else a police officer. Each boy had a dream, however distant it might have seemed.



He listened carefully before asking, "And how will you reach those places if you never learn to read? How will you understand the world, pursue your dreams, or make your own decisions?"



This time, nobody answered. Some lowered their eyes. A few seemed disappointed, perhaps because they had begun to recognise the distance between their dreams and their present circumstances.



The young man noticed their silence. His voice softened.



"Don't lose heart. You still have time. You can learn, you can study, and you can become what you wish to become. But you must take the first step. The treasure I promised to tell you about is hidden in this very book. It is called education."



He paused, allowing his words to settle.



"Gold can be stolen, wealth can disappear, and even the greatest possessions can be lost. But what you learn becomes a part of you. Education gives you the ability to question, to understand, to distinguish between right and wrong, and to make choices for yourself. It opens doors that you might not even know exist."



He looked at the boys and added, "A life without education is like a plate of rice without salt. It may keep you alive, but it loses something essential. The world is full of possibilities, but you must first learn how to recognise them."



That afternoon, the boys went home with something different from what they had expected. They had come looking for a story about buried gold and returned with a question about their own lives.



In the days that followed, they began attending school. The change did not happen overnight. There were difficulties, distractions and moments when their old habits returned. Yet, gradually, books became less unfamiliar, letters began to form words, and those words opened windows to a world they had never properly encountered.



Years passed. The boys grew up, completed their schooling, and some continued their education through college. Their childhood dreams took different shapes, and life led each of them along a different road. They began to understand things that had once seemed beyond their reach.



Perhaps, one day, they remembered that afternoon, the old Bengali primer, and the young man who had promised them a hidden treasure.



They had once imagined treasure as something buried beneath the earth, waiting for someone fortunate enough to discover it. With time, they understood that the greatest treasures are sometimes placed within our reach, yet remain invisible until someone teaches us how to recognise their value.



And so, they finally discovered the treasure. It had been waiting for them in the pages of a book all along.
